Chokecherry is one of the most common wild cherries across Montréal — thickets of it line paths, fences, and riverbanks. The giveaway is the fruit habit: instead of single cherries, the small dark fruits hang in long **grape-like racemes**. Named for the astringent "choke" when you bite a raw one — but cooked and strained, it makes a gorgeous deep-red jelly and syrup.

## Harvest
- Pick whole clusters once the cherries are **dark purple to nearly black** and slightly soft — late July through September here.
- Strip the fruit off the stalks. It's fiddly but fast once a cluster is fully ripe.
- Taste one for ripeness (spit the pit): ripe flesh is sweet up front, then puckery. Under-ripe red fruit is harshly astringent — leave it.

## Prep
- **Chokecherry jelly / syrup** is the classic: simmer the whole fruit with a little water, then strain through a jelly bag so the stones and skins are left behind. Cook the strained juice with sugar (and pectin — chokecherry is only moderately high) for jelly, or reduce for syrup.
- Also good in wild-fruit blends, sauces for game, or fruit leather (strained purée only — **never** pass the pits through).
- Deep colour and a distinctive almond-tinged cherry flavour.

## Safety
- **Do not crush or eat the pits.** Like other stone fruit in the *Prunus* family, the seeds are cyanogenic. Whole-fruit cooking and straining removes them — that's the whole point of the method.
- Everything green (leaves, twigs) is also cyanogenic; only the ripe flesh is food.

## Notes
- Distinguish from black cherry (*Prunus serotina*), which is a tall tree with similar racemes — both are prepared the same way, so an ID mix-up here is low-stakes as long as you cook and strain.
